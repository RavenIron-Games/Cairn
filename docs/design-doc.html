Cairn

On a world with no map and no portals, knowing where you are is knowledge. Cairn makes that knowledge something you can stack, light, and lose — without ever drawing it on the screen.

64 m Where vanilla smoke stops existing. Not culled — never emitted.
30 m The raven's altitude floor. Absent on every coastline.
4 m The footprint that separates a cairn from a wall. Shape, not piece count.
420 m Where the first fire was seen from. Down a chain of fifteen.
the problem

Knowledge that lives in screenshots

Ravenrest runs with no map and no portals. That is the whole appeal: the world is large, and getting anywhere is a thing you did rather than a thing you clicked. But the knowledge that makes it navigable already exists — it just lives in players' heads, and in screenshots pasted into Discord with a caption like the copper is north of the two rocks.

Every instinct says: draw it on the screen. Put a marker there. Share a pin. That instinct is the one thing this mod may never act on, and the reason is not taste. A HUD was cut from Ragnarok's Wrath deliberately, and a navigation mod is exactly the shape of thing that would quietly reintroduce it under a different name.

So the design constraint came first, and everything else is downstream of it.

house rule Inherited from RW; the escape hatch is welded shut on purpose.

Every answer is an object in the world

If a player learns where they are, they learn it by looking at terrain, a stone, a fire, a bird, or the sky. The moment the mod renders a bearing, it has become the companion app that was deliberately cut.

This is a harder rule than it sounds, because the game gives you four candidate channels and three of them turn out to be structurally unfit. Finding that out cost one afternoon with a decompiler and saved a month of building the wrong mod.

What follows is each channel, and what the shipping assembly actually said about it.

measured rejected Channel 1 of 4 — smoke.

Smoke is capped at 64 metres, by construction

The obvious beacon is a big fire throwing a column of smoke. It is also the first idea the engine kills, and it kills it three separate ways.

Past 64 m the smoke is not faded, not culled, not simplified. It is never emitted. No render setting, no hardware, and no config value reaches behind that gate.

Two more facts finish the argument. Smoke is globally capped — over 100 live puffs and FadeOldest() starts eating them — so a bigger column steals from every other fire the player can see. And each puff is a Rigidbody with a ten-second life, so scaling one up is a physics bill, not a rendering one. The engine even has FadeMostDistant(), which culls the furthest smoke first: vanilla's own policy is the precise opposite of what a beacon needs.

Decision: Cairn never uses vanilla smoke. Locked, with the evidence attached, so it does not get relitigated in six months by someone who reasonably assumes a fire makes smoke.

measured demoted Channel 2 of 4 — Hugin.

The raven cannot point

Hugin is almost too good to be true: a bird, already in the game, already themed to the studio's own name, that speaks to you in a rendered chat bubble rather than a UI panel. The first design had it circling toward the nearest landmark you had stood at.

The game will not do that, and the reasons are structural rather than incidental.

Below 30 metres of altitude the raven has no legal ground to land on. It is therefore absent on exactly the coastlines, river mouths and beaches where a navigator most needs help. Selection is a 15 m proximity search, not a distant beacon. And it despawns when a hostile is within 10 m or when RandEventSystem.InEvent() is true — meaning Ragnarok's Wrath's own Devastating Storms silence the bird at the exact moment you would most want it.

Decision: the raven is flavour, never load-bearing. It becomes the voice of a landmark once you have reached it — registering static texts, never overwriting vanilla tutorial text, degrading to silence. A good mod is allowed a grace note. It is not allowed to route its core function through one.

measured the design Channel 3 of 4 — the beacon.

The beacon must not be an object

What survives is a light burning at a marked place, visible across a valley or from the water. The trap here is subtler than the smoke gate, because the naive implementation looks right and fails silently at range.

Valheim streams objects by zone. Whether a client instantiates a thing at all depends on a flag baked into its prefab:

Vanilla fireplaces are near-only, so at beacon range the fire object is not instantiated on the client at all. And even a distant-flagged object is sent last and starves on a busy server. Any design that says "make the fire visible from further away" is fighting the object streamer, and loses.

Decision: the beacon is drawn client-side from synced state, never from a networked object. The server holds the truth — there is a lit cairn at these coordinates — and pushes it; the client draws the light there whether or not a single ZDO from that zone is loaded. This is not a new pattern: it is the path Ragnarok's Wrath already runs and verified in-game for plague fog. Existence is server truth, rendering is local, and the two never depend on each other.

unmeasured The one question that can still invalidate the design.

Fog is the only thing left that can kill it

Streaming is solved and geometry is generous. What remains is atmospheric: Valheim blends RenderSettings.fogDensity every frame from the current environment's four day-phase values, all of them serialized in prefabs rather than written in code. There is no number to look up. It has to be measured.

Under exponential-squared fog, transmittance falls as exp(-(density × distance)²) — and squaring makes density brutally decisive. One order of magnitude separates a beacon that reads at 400 m from one that does not exist.

The probe in tools\probe\ logs the live values across Clear, Misty, Rain and ThunderStorm for one in-game day. If Clear's 10% horizon falls short of 400 m, the beacon is not the primary channel and this document needs rewriting before the code exists.

If it falls short only in fog and rain, that is almost certainly the right behaviour rather than a defect — a beacon lost in weather is honest — but it has to be a decision, not a surprise.

One honest caveat about this whole section. It was written when the beacon was a column of grey smoke, which fog erases by definition — both are the same pale wash and contrast is all a plume has. A light is a different proposition: a bright point against a dark hillside carries where smoke dies, and at night it carries a long way indeed. So the measurement above may have stopped being the gate it was designed to be. That is a reason to take it deliberately and re-read the answer against the new design — not a reason to quietly let it lapse, which is how a gate becomes a formality.

reversed RavenIron's call, 2026-09-02. This document argued the opposite.

A cairn is a pile you build

The original design made a named sign the landmark and a column of smoke the beacon. It was wrong in a way that is obvious once said aloud: a cairn is a pile of rocks with a light on top. That is what the word means, and it does both jobs at once — the thing you steer by at distance is the same thing you read when you arrive.

So the pile marks the place and carries the light. A sign names it. Both halves stand alone: a sign with no pile is a named place without a light, a pile with no sign is a lit waymark nobody has named yet, and the ledger holds both.

No new prefabs, still. A mod that adds one must ship server-side or ZNetScene.CreateObjectsSorted calls DestroyZDO on every hash it cannot resolve — silent data loss, in someone else's world. So the pile is not a piece we provide. It is a pattern we recognise in what players already build out of ordinary stone.

Which raises the only hard question in the mod: how do you tell a waymark from a wall? Not by counting pieces — a tidy five-piece wall has a perfectly respectable piece count. By footprint, because that is the thing a cairn cannot fake:

What was builtPiecesFootprintVerdict
A stacked waymark30.4 mcairn
A low wall58 mnot a cairn
A stone house258 mnot a cairn

Every row there is a test, and removing the footprint rule fails two of them. Grouping runs as single-linkage clustering through a spatial grid rather than an all-pairs sweep — a world's stone runs to thousands of pieces, and O(n²) inside a tick is how a sweep becomes a stutter.

The pleasing part is what falls out for free: everyone's cairn looks different, none of them are ours, and with the mod uninstalled the whole thing degrades into exactly what it appears to be.

decided The pile marks it. The sign says what it is.

And a sign is what gives it a name

A light tells you there. It cannot tell you what. Vanilla already ships the thing that can: Sign is a TextReceiver with its text persisted in the ZDO under a revision counter, the author captured as a platform id, a 50-character limit, and the game's own text-entry flow. Set one into the side of a cairn and the place has a name.

Pairing is deliberately forgiving and deliberately flat — the nearest named sign within a few metres, measured on the horizontal plane only. A sign set into a cairn's flank sits well below its crown, and a 3D distance check would push it out of reach for no reason a player could see. That is not a hypothetical: the same mistake made Ragnarok's Wrath's announcements invisible to anyone standing on a hill, and it took two rounds of fixing the wrong end to find.

Cairn notices what players build. It never seeds the world with points of interest. A world where nobody has stacked anything has no landmarks, and that is correct.

retracted Kept because the mistake is the lesson.

A measurement that was wrong

This document's whole claim is that the design was settled by measurement rather than taste. So here is the measurement that lied, and what gave it away.

Prefab names live in asset bundles, not the assemblies, so they cannot be decompiled — and the sweep needs to know what a sign is called. A ZDO stores its prefab as a stable hash of that name, and a world save stores ZDOs. So: compute the hash of each candidate, search two real saves for those four bytes, and read off which names are real. It worked. sign and sign_notext both appeared; three other guesses did not; a known-good prefab appeared as a control. The result was written up as confirmed.

It was wrong. Re-run against a freshly saved test world, the same control returned zero, and no plain text appeared in that file either. The save format defeats raw byte scanning, so the original hits were never prefab data at all.

The tell had been sitting in the first run and was rationalised away: the control prefab returned one and two hits in worlds that certainly contained more of it than that. A control that disagrees with itself between two files has failed, whatever the other rows say. Reasoning about why the count seemed low is exactly the move that turns a broken instrument into a confident wrong answer.

The replacement does not need trusting: a console command that reads the loaded scene's own prefab list. There was always an authority available; the mistake was preferring a clever offline measurement to asking it.

What the world said

All of it was built and run on a dedicated server, against cairns a person stacked by hand rather than fixtures a test invented.

The occlusion row is the one that mattered most, and the proof is that it discriminates. A broken raycast gives all-hidden or all-lit; a ragged pattern with near cairns dark and a far one burning is what real ground produces. A beacon that shone through a mountain would have been a waypoint marker in a costume, and it would have been the easiest failure in the whole project to never notice — because by then everything else already looked right.

four theories A companion piece to the measurement that lied.

The bird cost four wrong answers

The raven was the smallest feature in the mod and took the longest, and every hour of it went the same way: a confident, plausible theory, killed by one line of measurement.

First, that no raven existed in the world — reasonable, since the prefab is nowhere in the scene's list. The diagnostic said raven in scene : True. Second, that appending our line politely to the end of vanilla's queue would work. It could never: the selector returns the first match and stops, four untriggered tutorials sat ahead of it, and being maximally deferential is precisely what guaranteed silence. The politeness was the bug. Third, that planted meant registered — it only ever meant we had made a GameObject, and three round trips vanished into the gap between our half of the exchange and vanilla's.

Fourth was right, and only because by then the instrument reported the game's verdict rather than our intention. Valheim has two ravens. m_instance is whichever spawned, and the selector discards any line whose m_munin flag differs from the bird's own — before distance, before priority, before anything. The world's bird was Munin. A correctly registered, correctly positioned line was thrown away by a boolean while every other signal read healthy.

The lesson is not "measure more". It is what to measure. Three instruments reported our own state and none of them could find this; the one that worked asked vanilla's selector what it had chosen and printed the answer. GetBestText : OURS is not an opinion about whether the thing should work.

The question that was left open, and when it came due

This document used to end with one thing deliberately unresolved. A named sign is player-authored text, and Cairn gives it reach it never had: a name only a visitor could read might now be spoken aloud, or hung on a light visible across a valley. The note said decide before any name leaves the sign it was written on, and left it there.

That moment arrived with the raven. A bird saying a player's words out loud is the name leaving the sign, so the question stopped being hypothetical and had to be answered in the same commit that built the voice.

The answer is a split: storage stays faithful, display decides. The ledger keeps exactly what was typed, rich-text tags and all — it is a record, and a record that quietly edits itself is worse than one that holds something awkward. Everything on the way out strips markup, so no name reaching a raven's mouth carries colour tags or size tags or anything that could paint over the rest of the dialogue.

The stripper is narrow on purpose, and its tests are the argument: a cairn called <-- the ford survives being spoken, so does cost <3 stone, and a tag cannot span a line break. Ordinary prose almost never contains <word>; being greedier would eat text somebody meant to keep. That the rule can differ per channel is the whole benefit of putting it at the display end rather than the storage end.

closing

The escape hatch stays closed

When a feature is hard to make legible, there is always one move available: draw it on the screen. Put the distance in a corner. Add a little arrow. It works every time, and it is how every mod of this kind ends up as a HUD with a story attached.

So the rule for this mod was written down before the first line of code: when something is hard to see, make it bigger or louder — never draw it. A taller stack. A brighter fire. A sound that carries past the fog. If none of those work, the feature does not ship.

That is a constraint that costs something, and it is the entire reason the mod is worth building. Everything in this document that changed — the anchor, the beacon, one retracted measurement — changed underneath that rule and not around it.
